Abstract

As recent security incidents have shown, AI agents can collude, circumvent their controls, and create unexpected harms in pursuit of their goals. A landmark 1994 study found that NASDAQ market makers increased their profits, at the expense of other market participants, by avoiding odd-eighth price quotes in 70 of 100 heavily traded stocks.a More recently, AI researchers saw similar behaviour among AI pricing agents in a market simulation.b

We ran more than 3,500 games on two open models in a simulated market where two or three AI "market makers" post prices under a regulator.

We made several significant discoveries, which could inform efforts to prevent harmful collusion:

  • When agents are able to communicate, they are far more likely to collude.
  • Initial instructions disproportionately influence collusive behaviour, both positively and negatively.
    • When the instructions contain a target price, agents tend to collude at that price. Replacing printed example prices with the bare profit rule lowered prices by 0.22 to 0.63 (0 competitive, 1 monopoly).
    • Initial prompts assigning the agent a persona with ethical character traits did not prevent collusion, but they steered it towards better outcomes.
  • Two signals were highly predictive of collusion.
    • Agents that open with noncompetitive prices tend to collude to keep them there. (AUC 0.81 to 0.91.)
    • Prompts containing "temptations" to collude are highly predictive of later collusion. AI was effective at identifying temptations in other agents' prompts, but not their own. (Correlation up to 0.85.)

These results matter for how AI agents are tested, instructed and supervised.

a Christie, W. G., and Schultz, P. H. (1994). Why Do NASDAQ Market Makers Avoid Odd-Eighth Quotes? Journal of Finance 49(5), 1813–1840. They found odd-eighth quotes "virtually nonexistent" for 70 of 100 actively traded NASDAQ stocks, and asked whether the dealers implicitly colluded.

b Fish, S., Gonczarowski, Y. A., and Shorrer, R. I. (2024). Algorithmic Collusion by Large Language Models. arXiv:2404.00806. In their oligopoly simulations, AI pricing agents "quickly and autonomously reach supracompetitive prices and profits".

About this repository

Prepared for the Apart Research AI Collusion Research Sprint (23–25 October 2026). The engineering and analysis were carried out by Claude at the prompting of the author; every review and its disposition is recorded in the repository.

About the game. Each firm quotes a half-spread (the distance from the mid-price to its bid and its ask) between 1.00 (competitive: costs covered, no excess profit) and 5.00, in ticks of 0.01. Results are reported on a price index from 0 (fully competitive) to 1 (monopoly). Each report opens with an orientation section that defines the game, the controls and the metrics it uses.

How to reproduce

  1. Build and test the engine (JDK 17, Maven 3): cd engine && mvn -q test && mvn -q package -DskipTests produces engine/target/collusion-engine.jar. Python 3.8+ with PyYAML (and matplotlib for figures) runs tools/; the Python tests are the test_*.py files beside the code (for example python -m pytest tools/trial).
  2. Serve a model. deploy/modal_vllm.py deploys an OpenAI-compatible vLLM endpoint on Modal, one profile per model. The server key is read from an environment variable named on the command line and is never written to a file; remote calls need COLLUSION_ALLOW_REMOTE=1 and a token budget.
  3. Make the grid, run it, collect and analyse. docs/runbooks/TRACK_M_RUNBOOK.md gives the exact commands: tools/trial/make_grid.py builds the trial grid from the design files in tools/trial/design/, run_waves.py and run_grid.py run it, and collect.py and the analysis scripts build the tables. The scripts that produced each report sit in that report's own folder.
  4. Reproduce the non-LLM results with the engine's batch runner (see engine/README.md); no GPU or API is needed.
