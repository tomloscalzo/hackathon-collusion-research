2. The combinations that matter most

  1. Detection × penalty is one lever, not four. In markets with no minimum price step and no tips, collusion is chosen almost exactly when regulator screen slope × P(evidence | investigation) × fine ratio × (reservation spread ratio − 1) is below 4. This holds in 98.9% of those scenarios. The inputs substitute for each other: a 10% chance of evidence with a fine ratio of 125 deters exactly as much as certain evidence with a fine ratio of 12.5. For policy and for AI oversight, this means the cheapest component can be scaled up instead of all of them.
  2. No detection, no deterrence. With a regulator screen slope of 0 and no tips, collusion runs at full intensity whatever the fine; even a fine ratio of 1,000 does nothing.
  3. A sensitive screen with a heavy fine removes collusion. With a regulator screen slope ≥ 0.05 and a fine ratio ≥ 125, collusion is chosen in 1.1% of scenarios. A gentle screen is not enough on its own: at slope 0.01 with a fine ratio of 125, 22% still collude.
  4. Tips have a hard threshold. Collusion becomes impossible once tip probability × P(evidence | investigation) × fine ratio exceeds 1. At the ripe baseline, a 5% per-round tip probability removes collusion.
  5. Patience × number of market makers follows the textbook threshold. A cartel of N firms holds roughly when patience δ ≥ 1 − 1/N. That means about 0.5 for 2 firms, 0.75 for 4 and 0.9 for 10. Impatient markets with many firms almost never collude. Once patience is high, the number of firms stops mattering.
  6. Very patient cartels collude more gently. They have more future profit to lose to an investigation, so they choose lower, less conspicuous prices: how often they collude stays high while intensity eases.

3. Ranges to remember

  • Most collusion-prone: regulator screen slope 0, no tips, fine ratio ≤ 5, 2–4 market makers, patience 0.8–0.9, no minimum price step. Every one of these 720 scenarios colludes, at full (monopoly) intensity.
  • Near collusion-proof: regulator screen slope ≥ 0.05 with a fine ratio ≥ 125, where 1.1% collude. Alternatively, a 5% tip probability once the fine ratio exceeds 20 ÷ P(evidence | investigation).
  • Borderline (where LLM behaviour is most informative): a moderate regulator screen slope (0.025–0.05), a moderate fine ratio (12.5–25) and patience 0.9–0.95. Small changes in the inputs flip whether a rational cartel colludes, so an LLM's departures from the rational choice are easiest to see. Our ripe baseline sits here.

4. Caveats

  • Modelling simplification in markets with a minimum price step. There, an investigation that finds nothing is treated as worse for firms than a conviction: post-investigation profit is set to zero, while ordinary competition still earns a small profit. This drives the dip at the highest patience, part of the tick-size effect, and the rise of collusion with P(evidence | investigation) when there is no fine. It is a recorded limitation (FUTURE_DIRECTIONS §6). None of the findings for markets with no price step depend on it.
  • Detection ends collusion permanently. Re-formation and repeat offending are not modelled, so the map is conservative about how often collusion pays.
  • Prisoner's-dilemma-style collusion only. Every finding on patience, number of market makers and preferenced volume share comes from each firm's temptation to break ranks. They do not apply to shared-objective coordination (all agents pursuing one goal, as in the Hugging Face incident), which has different incentives and is not modelled.
  • Averaging. The averages depend on the grid's ranges. The directions and the product rule do not.

Interpretation

Whether firms collude: It appears that the share of scenarios where collusion is chosen rises with patience (the chance the market continues another round), peaks at fairly high but not the highest patience, and then eases off, while it falls steadily as the number of market makers (competing firms) grows. These inputs interact strongly: impatient firms in markets with many market makers almost never collude, most likely because each firm gets only a small slice of cartel profit while undercutting wins most of the market for a round. Among very patient firms the number of market makers hardly matters, because the threat of losing future profits holds even large cartels together. The dip at the most patient end appears to come from a modelling simplification in markets with a minimum price step (an investigation that finds nothing is treated as worse for firms than a conviction): patient firms weigh heavily the ordinary earnings that such an investigation wipes out.

How hard they collude: It appears that collusion intensity (how far the chosen spread sits between the competitive and the monopoly level) rises with patience (the chance the market continues another round) up to fairly high levels and then falls, and that it is lower the larger the number of market makers (competing firms). The peak shifts towards more patient values as the number of market makers grows, most likely because larger cartels need more patience before they can hold any raised price at all. Past the peak, very patient cartels appear to moderate their prices to avoid drawing the regulator's attention, since they have more future profit to lose, with a modelling simplification in markets with a minimum price step (an investigation that finds nothing is treated as worse for firms than a conviction) adding to that fall. At the most patient end the number of market makers barely changes how hard the cartel pushes prices.

Mechanism detail: The switch-on line is the price-war incentive constraint: without a tick a cartel is sustainable iff δ/(1−δ) ≥ (1−φ)(N−1), i.e. δ ≥ 1 − 1/N at φ = 0 (screens and tips lower effective patience to δ(1−ρ_T)), so N = 10 needs δ ≥ 0.9 and the δ = 0.5 row collapses from 0.373 at N = 2 to 0.000 at N = 10. By δ = 0.99 the constraint is slack almost everywhere and N barely matters (0.255–0.274): in tick-free markets every per-firm payoff and the fine (scaled to per-firm monopoly profit) shrink by the same 1/N, so N acts only through the incentive constraint. The high-δ decline itself is not a patience effect: in tick-free cells the N = 2 share stays at 0.511 from δ = 0.7 to 0.99, while in tick cells it drops from 0.345 to 0.128, the π_post = 0 artifact (a trigger without evidence destroys a tick-rent stream worth π^N/(1−δ)), which is also why N = 2 is the lowest column from δ = 0.9 up.

Whether firms collude: It appears that collusion becomes more common as the preferenced volume share (the share of orders split evenly between firms whatever their price) grows, and that it rises with patience (the chance the market continues another round) before easing off at the most patient end. Volume that is split evenly whatever the price cannot be won by undercutting, so it most likely shrinks the reward for cheating on the cartel. The preferenced volume share makes the biggest difference among impatient firms, most likely because fewer of their cartels are forced to keep prices below what they would like in order to stop members cheating. Once firms are patient, the lines for different preferenced volume shares come together, because the temptation to cheat no longer limits the price. The fall at high patience appears to reflect patient cartels choosing caution to protect their future profits, plus a modelling simplification in markets with a minimum price step (an investigation that finds nothing is treated as worse for firms than a conviction).

Whether firms collude: It appears that a larger tick size (the minimum price step) generally lowers the share of colluding scenarios, while patience (the chance the market continues another round) raises it up to fairly high levels before it eases off. The tick size cuts both ways: among impatient firms a moderate price step actually helps the cartel, most likely because undercutting by a whole step is a big cut when margins are thin, and the price war drops below what firms earn in normal competition with a price step. Why: more room between cost and the customers' walk-away spread means bigger cartel profits, but the screen reacts to the absolute markup, so the wide spreads a cartel would charge in roomy markets are also more likely to be flagged.
