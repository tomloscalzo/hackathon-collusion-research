Interpretation

Whether firms collude: It appears that collusion falls as the number of market makers (competing firms) grows and rises with the preferenced volume share (the share of orders split evenly between firms whatever their price). The preferenced volume share helps most when there are many market makers, where the temptation to undercut is greatest, because evenly split volume cannot be won by cheating. With very few market makers it makes almost no difference, or even hurts slightly, most likely because in small markets with a minimum price step a higher preferenced volume share raises ordinary competitive earnings, leaving less space to collude and more exposure to a modelling simplification for such markets (an investigation that finds nothing is treated as worse for firms than a conviction).

How hard they collude: It appears that collusion intensity (how far the chosen spread sits between the competitive and the monopoly level) falls with the number of market makers (competing firms) and rises with the preferenced volume share (the share of orders split evenly between firms whatever their price), and the lines fan out: the preferenced volume share barely matters with very few market makers but makes a large difference when there are many. This is most likely because it eases the temptation to cheat, so fewer large cartels are forced to hold prices below the level they would prefer.

Details
Share where collusion is chosen (left chart)

Raising the number of market makers from 2 to 10 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.35 to 0.19. Why: each member's share of the cartel profit is 1/N, while a one-round undercut still captures most of the contestable flow, so the temptation to cheat grows with N.

Raising the preferenced volume share from 0 to 0.6 raises the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.26 to 0.33. Why: preferenced flow is shared whatever the price, so an undercut steals less volume and cheating pays less.

Highest: 0.36 at N = 3, φ = 0.6; lowest: 0.14 at N = 10, φ = 0.

The two interact: going from the lowest to the highest N changes the value by -0.22 when φ = 0 but by -0.08 when φ = 0.6.

Mechanism detail: Preferencing helps most where cheating temptation is greatest: at N = 10 the share rises from 0.143 to 0.258 with φ, while at N = 2 it is flat or slightly negative (0.361 → 0.339). Since the threshold is (1−φ)(N−1) ≤ δ/(1−δ), φ matters only where N makes the constraint bind; the share of cells where any collusion is sustainable at N = 10 rises from 0.388 to 0.679. At N = 2 two things offset: in tick-free cells φ still helps (0.459 → 0.511), but in tick cells it hurts (0.312 → 0.253), likely because with two firms more preferencing raises the static-Nash tick margin (j* from 1 to 2–3), shrinking the collusive range and enlarging the π^N stream exposed to the π_post = 0 artifact.

Chosen collusion intensity θ* (right chart)

Raising the number of market makers from 2 to 10 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.27 to 0.12. Why: each member's share of the cartel profit is 1/N, while a one-round undercut still captures most of the contestable flow, so the temptation to cheat grows with N.

Raising the preferenced volume share from 0 to 0.6 raises the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.17 to 0.24. Why: preferenced flow is shared whatever the price, so an undercut steals less volume and cheating pays less.

Highest: 0.28 at N = 3, φ = 0.6; lowest: 0.08 at N = 10, φ = 0.

Mechanism detail: θ* fans out the same way: flat at N = 2 (0.264–0.267) and nearly doubling at N = 10 (0.080 → 0.159). In tick-free markets N and φ enter only through the incentive constraint, so φ's gain comes from relaxing it: more cells collude and fewer are capped below their preferred margin (the share of cells in the constraint-binding interior regime falls from 0.066 at φ = 0 to 0.046 at φ = 0.6).
