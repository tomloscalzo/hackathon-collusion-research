Interpretation

Whether firms collude: It appears that collusion falls steeply as the fine ratio (the fine as a multiple of a firm's full-collusion profit for a round) rises and also falls as the number of market makers (competing firms) grows, with these acting nearly independently. This seems to be because the fine is measured against each firm's own full-collusion profit, so adding firms does not make a given fine ratio heavier per firm. The number of market makers therefore acts mainly through the temptation to cheat, while the fine ratio acts on whether colluding is worth the risk at all.

How hard they collude: It appears that collusion intensity (how far the chosen spread sits between the competitive and the monopoly level) falls as the fine ratio (the fine as a multiple of a firm's full-collusion profit for a round) rises at every number of market makers (competing firms) and is lower with more firms, but the gap between small and large cartels narrows sharply under high fine ratios. This is most likely because heavy fines push surviving cartels to low prices, where the risk of investigation and the strain on the cartel are small, or leave only scenarios where no investigation can ever start, so the number of market makers has less to act on.

Details
Share where collusion is chosen (left chart)

Raising the number of market makers from 2 to 10 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.35 to 0.19. Why: each member's share of the cartel profit is 1/N, while a one-round undercut still captures most of the contestable flow, so the temptation to cheat grows with N.

Raising the fine ratio from 0 to 500 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.67 to 0.05. Why: bigger fines raise the expected cost of being convicted.

Highest: 0.80 at N = 2, fine ratio = 0; lowest: 0.03 at N = 10, fine ratio = 500.

The two interact: going from the lowest to the highest N changes the value by -0.36 when fine ratio = 0 but by -0.02 when fine ratio = 500.

Mechanism detail: Fines and N are close to separable: the fine is a phase-common penalty that cancels out of the cheating condition, so N works through the incentive constraint and the fine through participation (in tick-free cells collusion pays only if π_c(θ) > ρ_E(θ)·F at some θ). The N = 10 to N = 2 ratio moves only from 0.55 at no fine (0.446/0.804) to 0.61 at 500× (0.035/0.057). Because fineRatio is scaled to per-firm monopoly profit, more firms do not make a given fineRatio heavier per firm.

Chosen collusion intensity θ* (right chart)

Raising the number of market makers from 2 to 10 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.27 to 0.12. Why: each member's share of the cartel profit is 1/N, while a one-round undercut still captures most of the contestable flow, so the temptation to cheat grows with N.

Raising the fine ratio from 0 to 500 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.45 to 0.04. Why: bigger fines raise the expected cost of being convicted.

Highest: 0.62 at N = 2, fine ratio = 0; lowest: 0.03 at N = 10, fine ratio = 500.

The two interact: going from the lowest to the highest N changes the value by -0.39 when fine ratio = 0 but by -0.02 when fine ratio = 500.

Mechanism detail: θ* falls with the fine at every N (N = 2: 0.623 → 0.050), and the gap across N narrows sharply (0.623 vs 0.233 at no fine; 0.050 vs 0.029 at 500×). Likely reason: heavy fines push surviving cartels to low margins, where screen hazard and hence the strain on the incentive constraint are small, or leave only hazard-free cells, so N has less to bite on.
