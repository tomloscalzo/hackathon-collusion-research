Interpretation

Whether firms collude: It appears that collusion falls as the tip probability (the chance each round that an investigation starts from a tip, whatever the price) and P(evidence | investigation) (the chance an investigation produces a guilty finding) rise, and that these compound: a higher chance of a guilty finding deters more when tips are common. This is most likely because a tip can start an investigation at any price, so the risk of conviction applies whatever the cartel does, not only at high prices. The fall at the highest chances of a guilty finding is weaker than it would otherwise be, which appears to be a modelling simplification in markets with a minimum price step (an investigation that finds nothing is treated as worse for firms than a conviction).

How hard they collude: It appears that collusion intensity (how far the chosen spread sits between the competitive and the monopoly level) falls with both the tip probability (the chance each round that an investigation starts from a tip, whatever the price) and P(evidence | investigation) (the chance an investigation produces a guilty finding). A higher chance of a guilty finding most likely lowers intensity directly, since it raises the expected fine attached to each extra bit of price. Tips seem to lower intensity on average only because fewer cartels form, even though the cartels that remain tend to price higher.

Details
Share where collusion is chosen (left chart)

Raising the tip probability from 0 to 0.05 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.38 to 0.20. Why: tips end cartels at any margin, so they shorten the expected life of the cartel.

Raising the probability an investigation finds evidence from 0.1 to 1 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.39 to 0.23. Why: a higher chance that an investigation ends in a conviction makes the fine more likely to be paid.

Highest: 0.48 at ρ_tip = 0, P(evidence) = 0.1; lowest: 0.15 at ρ_tip = 0.05, P(evidence) = 1.

Caveat: patterns involving tick, patience or P(evidence) are partly shaped by the π_post = 0 calibration in tick markets (a recorded limitation).

Mechanism detail: Both raise the per-round expected penalty P(trigger) × P(evidence) × F, and they compound: raising P(evidence) from 0.1 to 1.0 lowers the share by 34% with no tip (0.482 → 0.318) but 48% with a 5% tip (0.282 → 0.146), because the tip makes a trigger possible at every margin, so conviction risk bites at every θ, not only at high markups. The fall from 0.6 to 1.0 at a 5% tip (0.158 → 0.146) is weaker than the tick-free fall (0.225 → 0.172) because tick cells partly reverse under the π_post = 0 artifact.

Chosen collusion intensity θ* (right chart)

Raising the tip probability from 0 to 0.05 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.25 to 0.14. Why: tips end cartels at any margin, so they shorten the expected life of the cartel.

Raising the probability an investigation finds evidence from 0.1 to 1 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.26 to 0.17. Why: a higher chance that an investigation ends in a conviction makes the fine more likely to be paid.

Highest: 0.31 at ρ_tip = 0, P(evidence) = 0.1; lowest: 0.11 at ρ_tip = 0.05, P(evidence) = 1.

Caveat: patterns involving tick, patience or P(evidence) are partly shaped by the π_post = 0 calibration in tick markets (a recorded limitation).

Mechanism detail: θ* falls with both (0.309 at no tip and P(evidence) 0.1; 0.109 at 5% and 1.0). On average the tip lowers θ* even though, given participation, it raises the margin; P(evidence) unambiguously lowers it because it multiplies the marginal penalty of extra markup.
