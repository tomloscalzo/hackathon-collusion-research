Interpretation

Whether firms collude: It appears that collusion falls with both the tip probability (the chance each round that an investigation starts from a tip, whatever the price) and the fine ratio (the fine as a multiple of a firm's full-collusion profit for a round), and that together they can end collusion entirely. A tip creates a minimum expected fine that no pricing choice can avoid, so once tips are common and fines are heavy, the expected fine most likely exceeds even the best profit a cartel could earn. Without tips, some collusion survives heavy fines, apparently in scenarios where the regulator's screen (its rule for opening investigations when spreads are wide) is switched off, so nothing ever starts an investigation.

How hard they collude: It appears that collusion intensity (how far the chosen spread sits between the competitive and the monopoly level) falls with the fine ratio (the fine as a multiple of a firm's full-collusion profit for a round) and with the tip probability (the chance each round that an investigation starts from a tip, whatever the price), disappearing entirely when tips are common and fines are heavy. With no fine, tips barely change intensity, most likely because a tip then only shortens the cartel's life: some cartels no longer form, but those that remain price higher, and these roughly cancel.

Details
Share where collusion is chosen (left chart)

Raising the tip probability from 0 to 0.05 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.38 to 0.20. Why: tips end cartels at any margin, so they shorten the expected life of the cartel.

Raising the fine ratio from 0 to 500 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.67 to 0.05. Why: bigger fines raise the expected cost of being convicted.

Highest: 0.71 at ρ_tip = 0, fine ratio = 0; lowest: 0.00 at ρ_tip = 0.05, fine ratio = 250.

Mechanism detail: The tip creates an unavoidable penalty floor: whatever the margin, the expected fine per round is at least ρ_tip × P(evidence) × F, while the best possible per-round profit is monopoly profit π_c(1) = F/fineRatio. So collusion is impossible once ρ_tip × P(evidence) × fineRatio > 1 at every P(evidence) level, which is why a 5% tip gives exactly 0.000 at fines 250 and 500 (0.05 × 0.1 × 250 = 1.25). With no tip, the 500× share still holds at 0.125, largely cells with no screen, where nothing triggers and the fine is never paid.

Chosen collusion intensity θ* (right chart)

Raising the tip probability from 0 to 0.05 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.25 to 0.14. Why: tips end cartels at any margin, so they shorten the expected life of the cartel.

Raising the fine ratio from 0 to 500 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.45 to 0.04. Why: bigger fines raise the expected cost of being convicted.

Highest: 0.46 at ρ_tip = 0, fine ratio = 0; lowest: 0.00 at ρ_tip = 0.05, fine ratio = 250.

Mechanism detail: θ* follows (5% tip: 0.425 → 0.000; no tip: 0.465 → 0.104). At zero fine the tip barely changes θ* (0.465 vs 0.425), likely because without a fine a tip only shortens the cartel's life, which costs some participation but raises the margin of those that stay.
