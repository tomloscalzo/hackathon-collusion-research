Interpretation

Whether firms collude: It appears that collusion falls as the tip probability (the chance each round that an investigation starts from a tip, whatever the price) rises and as the tick size (the minimum price step) grows, and that tips hurt proportionally more in markets with a price step. In markets with no price step, a tip costs expected fines and a shorter cartel life. In markets with a price step there is an extra cost from a modelling simplification for such markets (an investigation that finds nothing is treated as worse for firms than a conviction), since a tip-started investigation that finds nothing most likely also wipes out ordinary competitive earnings; when every investigation ends in a conviction, tips hurt about equally with or without a price step.

How hard they collude: It appears that collusion intensity (how far the chosen spread sits between the competitive and the monopoly level) falls with both the tip probability (the chance each round that an investigation starts from a tip, whatever the price) and the tick size (the minimum price step). Markets with no price step are least affected by tips, most likely because there the cartels that still form respond to tips by pricing higher, since hiding helps little when a whistleblower may expose them anyway, which partly offsets the cartels lost.

Details
Share where collusion is chosen (left chart)

Raising the tick size from 0 to 0.5 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.38 to 0.20. Why: a coarser tick gives competitors a profit floor (tick rents), so colluding gains less over competing; in this calibration it also makes an inconclusive investigation costly (π_post = 0).

Raising the tip probability from 0 to 0.05 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.38 to 0.20. Why: tips end cartels at any margin, so they shorten the expected life of the cartel.

Highest: 0.47 at tick = 0, ρ_tip = 0; lowest: 0.12 at tick = 0.5, ρ_tip = 0.05.

Caveat: patterns involving tick, patience or P(evidence) are partly shaped by the π_post = 0 calibration in tick markets (a recorded limitation).

Mechanism detail: A tip hurts more in tick markets (−41% without a tick, 0.471 → 0.279; −55% with a 0.5 tick, 0.269 → 0.121). In tick-free markets a tip costs expected fines, a shorter life and a tighter incentive constraint; in tick markets each tip that finds no evidence also wipes out the tick-rent stream under π_post = 0. At P(evidence) = 1 the proportional tip effect is nearly equal across ticks (−53%, −56%, −54%), so the extra damage in the full average is the artifact.

Chosen collusion intensity θ* (right chart)

Raising the tick size from 0 to 0.5 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.26 to 0.14. Why: a coarser tick gives competitors a profit floor (tick rents), so colluding gains less over competing; in this calibration it also makes an inconclusive investigation costly (π_post = 0).

Raising the tip probability from 0 to 0.05 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.25 to 0.14. Why: tips end cartels at any margin, so they shorten the expected life of the cartel.

Highest: 0.29 at tick = 0, ρ_tip = 0; lowest: 0.09 at tick = 0.5, ρ_tip = 0.05.

Caveat: patterns involving tick, patience or P(evidence) are partly shaped by the π_post = 0 calibration in tick markets (a recorded limitation).

Mechanism detail: θ* falls with the tip in every column (tick 0: 0.294 → 0.211; tick 0.5: 0.189 → 0.086). The tick-free column is least affected because there, given participation, a tip raises the chosen margin (tick-free colluders at slope 0.01: 0.703 → 0.897), partly offsetting lost participation.
