Interpretation

Whether firms collude: It appears that collusion falls both as the number of market makers (competing firms) grows and as the regulator screen slope (how strongly the regulator's screen, its rule for opening investigations, reacts to wide spreads) rises, and these effects roughly multiply because they act through different channels: more firms make cheating more tempting, while the screen raises the risk of an investigation. The smallest numbers of market makers look almost identical, most likely because small cartels hold together easily. The penalty for having many market makers grows under steeper screens, apparently because the risk of investigation makes the future less certain, which weakens what keeps large cartels together.

How hard they collude: It appears that collusion intensity (how far the chosen spread sits between the competitive and the monopoly level) falls steeply as the regulator screen slope (how strongly the regulator's screen, its rule for opening investigations, reacts to wide spreads) rises at every number of market makers (competing firms), and is lower with more firms. With no screen, colluding firms most likely push straight to the full-collusion price. Under steep screens, cartels with many market makers cut intensity much more, apparently because high prices raise the risk of investigation, which in turn makes cheating more tempting, so large cartels must keep prices down to stay together.

Details
Share where collusion is chosen (left chart)

Raising the number of market makers from 2 to 10 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.35 to 0.19. Why: each member's share of the cartel profit is 1/N, while a one-round undercut still captures most of the contestable flow, so the temptation to cheat grows with N.

Raising the screen slope from 0 to 0.2 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.55 to 0.14. Why: a steeper screen makes high spreads more likely to trigger an investigation, so cartels must keep margins low or not collude.

Highest: 0.65 at N = 2, slope = 0; lowest: 0.08 at N = 10, slope = 0.2.

The two interact: going from the lowest to the highest N changes the value by -0.27 when slope = 0 but by -0.09 when slope = 0.2.

Mechanism detail: N and the screen work through different channels, so their effects nearly multiply: N raises cheating temptation, the screen raises the hazard and expected penalty. In tick-free markets the screen does not depend on N (it sees the absolute markup) and per-firm payoffs and fines all scale with 1/N, so N acts only through the incentive constraint, which is why N = 2 and 3 are almost identical (0.652 vs 0.637 at slope 0). The N penalty grows with the slope (N = 10 to N = 2 ratio 0.59 at slope 0, 0.48 at 0.2) because the screen hazard also lowers effective patience δ(1−ρ_T) in the constraint: the share of N = 10 cells where collusion is sustainable falls from 0.541 to 0.481 across slopes, while N = 2 stays at 0.915–0.921.

Chosen collusion intensity θ* (right chart)

Raising the number of market makers from 2 to 10 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.27 to 0.12. Why: each member's share of the cartel profit is 1/N, while a one-round undercut still captures most of the contestable flow, so the temptation to cheat grows with N.

Raising the screen slope from 0 to 0.2 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.53 to 0.06. Why: a steeper screen makes high spreads more likely to trigger an investigation, so cartels must keep margins low or not collude.

Highest: 0.65 at N = 2, slope = 0; lowest: 0.02 at N = 10, slope = 0.2.

The two interact: going from the lowest to the highest N changes the value by -0.30 when slope = 0 but by -0.07 when slope = 0.2.

Mechanism detail: θ* falls steeply with the slope for every N (N = 2: 0.650 → 0.094), since each extra unit of markup raises the screen hazard; without a screen colluders choose monopoly (θ* among N = 2 colluders is 0.998 at slope 0). Among colluders, large N cuts θ* more under steep screens (0.534 at N = 2 vs 0.269 at N = 10, slope 0.2): the screen hazard rises with θ, so the incentive constraint binds at high margins and caps θ* where cheating temptation is high (the internal-risk regime).
