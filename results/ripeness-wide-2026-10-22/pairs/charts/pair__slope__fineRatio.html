Interpretation

Whether firms collude: It appears that collusion falls as the regulator screen slope (how strongly the regulator's screen, its rule for opening investigations, reacts to wide spreads) and the fine ratio (the fine as a multiple of a firm's full-collusion profit for a round) rise, and this is the strongest interaction in the model. What seems to matter is the screen and the fine together: a gentle screen with a heavy fine deters about as much as a steep screen with a moderate fine. With no screen the fine works only through tips (investigations that start at random, whatever the price), so many cartels survive even heavy fines, but a steep screen combined with a heavy fine makes collusion all but disappear.

How hard they collude: It appears that collusion intensity (how far the chosen spread sits between the competitive and the monopoly level) falls with both the regulator screen slope (how strongly the regulator's screen, its rule for opening investigations, reacts to wide spreads) and the fine ratio (the fine as a multiple of a firm's full-collusion profit for a round). Without a screen, even a very high fine ratio leaves noticeable intensity, most likely because the surviving cartels there face no risk of investigation and push prices to the full-collusion level. Even with no fine, a screen alone lowers intensity, apparently because an investigation ends the cartel's future profits, so cartels trade some margin for a longer life.

Details
Share where collusion is chosen (left chart)

Raising the screen slope from 0 to 0.2 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.55 to 0.14. Why: a steeper screen makes high spreads more likely to trigger an investigation, so cartels must keep margins low or not collude.

Raising the fine ratio from 0 to 500 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.67 to 0.05. Why: bigger fines raise the expected cost of being convicted.

Highest: 0.74 at slope = 0, fine ratio = 0; lowest: 0.00 at slope = 0.1, fine ratio = 500.

The two interact: going from the lowest to the highest slope changes the value by -0.31 when fine ratio = 500 but by -0.14 when fine ratio = 0.

Mechanism detail: This is the sharpest interaction in the grid. A markup m earns rent of roughly m × Q/N but carries expected penalty slope × m × P(evidence) × F, so in tick-free cells with no tip collusion pays at small m iff slope × P(evidence) × fineRatio × (s̄/s₀ − 1) < 4 (verified in 98.9% of such cells; the rest are knife-edge cells with θ* < 0.001), and the share there depends only on the product slope × fine (slope 0.01 with fine 500, 0.1 with 50 and 0.2 with 25 all give 0.272). With no screen the fine works only through tips (0.741 → 0.307), and with slope ≥ 0.1 and fine ≥ 250 collusion all but disappears (≤ 0.004).

Chosen collusion intensity θ* (right chart)

Raising the screen slope from 0 to 0.2 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.53 to 0.06. Why: a steeper screen makes high spreads more likely to trigger an investigation, so cartels must keep margins low or not collude.

Raising the fine ratio from 0 to 500 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.45 to 0.04. Why: bigger fines raise the expected cost of being convicted.

Highest: 0.70 at slope = 0, fine ratio = 0; lowest: 0.00 at slope = 0.1, fine ratio = 500.

The two interact: going from the lowest to the highest slope changes the value by -0.43 when fine ratio = 0 but by -0.29 when fine ratio = 500.

Mechanism detail: Without a screen even a 500× fine leaves θ* at 0.293, because most colluders there are hazard-free cells that go to monopoly, while at slope 0.2 θ* is 0.000 from fine 250. At zero fine the screen alone lowers θ* from 0.697 to 0.263: the cartel trades margin for longevity even with no penalty, because a trigger ends the rents.
