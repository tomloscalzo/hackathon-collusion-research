Interpretation

Whether firms collude: It appears that collusion falls steeply as the fine ratio (the fine as a multiple of a firm's full-collusion profit for a round) rises and, on average, peaks at middle values of the reservation spread ratio (the room between cost and the spread at which customers stop trading; "room" below). With no fine, collusion rises with room, most likely only because markets with a minimum price step need space above normal prices. With a fine, a wider room becomes less attractive, apparently because the fine is measured against full-collusion profit, which grows with the room faster than the profit from a small, inconspicuous markup. Under the highest fine ratios collusion is low at every level of room.

How hard they collude: It appears that collusion intensity (how far the chosen spread sits between the competitive and the monopoly level) falls as the fine ratio (the fine as a multiple of a firm's full-collusion profit for a round) rises and peaks at a fairly low, but not the lowest, reservation spread ratio (the room between cost and the spread at which customers stop trading; "room" below) at every fine ratio. In markets with no minimum price step, intensity seems to fall steadily as the room widens even with no fine, most likely because the regulator's screen (its rule for opening investigations when spreads are wide) judges the absolute spread, so the same degree of collusion looks more suspicious in a wide market. Under high fine ratios the different values of room converge on low intensity.

Details
Share where collusion is chosen (left chart)

Raising the fine ratio from 0 to 500 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.67 to 0.05. Why: bigger fines raise the expected cost of being convicted.

The share of cells where collusion is chosen peaks at the reservation spread ratio = 5 (0.33) and is lower at both ends (0.24 at 2, 0.28 at 12). Why: more room between cost and the customers' walk-away spread means bigger cartel profits, but the screen reacts to the absolute markup, so the wide spreads a cartel would charge in roomy markets are also more likely to be flagged.

Highest: 0.76 at fine ratio = 0, s̄/s₀ = 12; lowest: 0.04 at fine ratio = 500, s̄/s₀ = 12.

The two interact: going from the lowest to the highest fine ratio changes the value by -0.72 when s̄/s₀ = 12 but by -0.39 when s̄/s₀ = 2.

Mechanism detail: At zero fine the share rises with s̄/s₀ (0.443 → 0.763) only because tick cells need room above the tick-rent margin; tick-free cells are at 0.741 for every s̄/s₀. With a fine the pattern turns down past s̄/s₀ = 3–5 because fineRatio is scaled to monopoly profit, which grows with the demand range faster than the rent from a small markup; relative to that rent the fine grows in proportion to (s̄/s₀ − 1), so tick-free shares fall monotonically (fine 25: 0.606 → 0.255). By 500× the rows are flat near 0.05, mostly hazard-free cells.

Chosen collusion intensity θ* (right chart)

Raising the fine ratio from 0 to 500 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.45 to 0.04. Why: bigger fines raise the expected cost of being convicted.

The mean collusion intensity θ* peaks at the reservation spread ratio = 3 (0.25) and is lower at both ends (0.20 at 2, 0.16 at 12). Why: more room between cost and the customers' walk-away spread means bigger cartel profits, but the screen reacts to the absolute markup, so the wide spreads a cartel would charge in roomy markets are also more likely to be flagged.

Highest: 0.55 at fine ratio = 0, s̄/s₀ = 3; lowest: 0.04 at fine ratio = 500, s̄/s₀ = 2.

Mechanism detail: θ* peaks at s̄/s₀ = 3 at every fine level (0.553 at zero fine) and falls beyond it; tick-free θ* falls monotonically even at zero fine (0.619 → 0.375), because the screen sees absolute markup, so the same θ is more conspicuous in a wide market. At high fines the columns converge (0.037–0.045 at 500×).
