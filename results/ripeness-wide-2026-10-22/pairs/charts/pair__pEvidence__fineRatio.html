Interpretation

Whether firms collude: It appears that collusion falls with both P(evidence | investigation) (the chance an investigation produces a guilty finding) and the fine ratio (the fine as a multiple of a firm's full-collusion profit for a round), because together they set the expected cost of each investigation; in markets with no minimum price step, a low chance of a guilty finding with a heavy fine deters about as much as a certain guilty finding with a lighter fine. With no fine, however, collusion rises with the chance of a guilty finding even though nothing is ever fined, which appears to be a modelling simplification in markets with a minimum price step (an investigation that finds nothing is treated as worse for firms than a conviction). Under heavy fines a small remnant of collusion remains, most likely in scenarios where no investigation can ever start.

How hard they collude: It appears that collusion intensity (how far the chosen spread sits between the competitive and the monopoly level) falls with the fine ratio (the fine as a multiple of a firm's full-collusion profit for a round) and, on average, with P(evidence | investigation) (the chance an investigation produces a guilty finding), which together set the expected cost of an investigation. Once the fine ratio is meaningful, a higher chance of a guilty finding lowers intensity sharply. With no fine, intensity instead rises with that chance, which seems to be a modelling simplification in markets with a minimum price step (an investigation that finds nothing is treated as worse for firms than a conviction) rather than real behaviour.

Details
Share where collusion is chosen (left chart)

Raising the probability an investigation finds evidence from 0.1 to 1 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.39 to 0.23. Why: a higher chance that an investigation ends in a conviction makes the fine more likely to be paid.

Raising the fine ratio from 0 to 500 lowers the share of cells where collusion is chosen from 0.67 to 0.05. Why: bigger fines raise the expected cost of being convicted.

Highest: 0.77 at P(evidence) = 1, fine ratio = 0; lowest: 0.03 at P(evidence) = 1, fine ratio = 500.

The two interact: going from the lowest to the highest P(evidence) changes the value by +0.15 when fine ratio = 0 but by -0.05 when fine ratio = 500.

Why the no-fine column rises: in the model a trigger ends the cartel whether or not evidence is found, so P(evidence) only decides what happens afterwards (fine plus normal competition, or π_post forever). With no fine, the only difference is π^N versus π_post. Split by tick, the no-fine share is flat at 0.74 when tick = 0 and rises only in tick markets (0.66 to 0.87 at tick 0.25; 0.45 to 0.69 at tick 0.5), where π_post = 0 < π^N.

Caveat: patterns involving tick, patience or P(evidence) are partly shaped by the π_post = 0 calibration in tick markets (a recorded limitation).

Mechanism detail: In tick-free markets these two enter only as the product P(evidence) × F, the expected fine per trigger: the share at P(evidence) 0.1 with fine 125 equals that at 1.0 with fine 12.5 (0.380), and 0.3 × 25 equals 0.6 × 12.5 (0.493). The full table breaks that symmetry because of tick cells: at zero fine collusion rises with P(evidence) (0.617 → 0.767) although nothing is fined, which is the π_post = 0 artifact (tick-free share 0.741 in every row at zero fine). The floor near 0.033 at P(evidence) = 1 and fines ≥ 250 is cells with no screen and no tip, where no trigger ever happens.

Chosen collusion intensity θ* (right chart)

Raising the probability an investigation finds evidence from 0.1 to 1 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.26 to 0.17. Why: a higher chance that an investigation ends in a conviction makes the fine more likely to be paid.

Raising the fine ratio from 0 to 500 lowers the mean collusion intensity θ* from 0.45 to 0.04. Why: bigger fines raise the expected cost of being convicted.

Highest: 0.52 at P(evidence) = 1, fine ratio = 0; lowest: 0.03 at P(evidence) = 1, fine ratio = 500.
